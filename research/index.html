Research

Most of what I work on comes back to one question: how much can you learn about a physical process from the signals it gives off while it runs, rather than from destroying the part afterward.

Virtual sensing for friction stir spot welding

Thesis work in Prof. Martin Jun's Multiscale Manufacturing Research Laboratory. Weld quality is normally confirmed by sectioning the weld and measuring it — which destroys the part and tells you nothing in real time. The question is whether the geometry that determines joint strength can be predicted instead, from signals available while the weld is being made.

The experimental base is a 27-run full-factorial design over plunge depth, spindle speed, and dwell time on AA6061-T6, with acoustic, machine-controller, and surface-profilometry data captured for every run. The modelling approach is a screened hybrid: regression on the process parameters first, then sparse selection over sensor features against the residual — so the sensors only earn their place where the parameters alone fall short. Validation is leave-one-out cross-validation against a response-surface baseline.

A second thread treats the weld acoustically as a sequence rather than a single event, classifying plunge, dwell, and retract stages from audio alone so the process can be segmented in real time.

Robot learning for manufacturing cells

Volunteer work with Prof. Yu She's group on vision-language-action models, and on world models that take video as input.

The interest is practical rather than general: setting up a new robotic cell is expensive mostly because of the programming and teaching time, and contact-rich tasks — insertion, screwdriving, anything with compliance in it — are exactly where hand-written motion planning is weakest. If learned policies can shorten the path from "new part" to "cell running," that changes the economics of small-batch and reconfigurable manufacturing more than faster hardware would.

Multimodal process monitoring

The broader thread underneath both of the above. Manufacturing processes emit far more than they are usually asked about — sound, vibration, current draw, thermal signature, surface texture — and most of it goes unrecorded. The interesting problems are in fusing those channels into something that holds up statistically, and in knowing when a sensor is genuinely adding information rather than fitting noise.
